An AI for Slay the Spire 2, judged by the real game

Spireline is building an AI that plays Slay the Spire 2 better than the best human players, Ironclad at Ascension 10 first. The AI learns on a fast copy of the game and is judged on the real game running headless on one PC. This paper is the plain-language record of what the project is, why it is built this way, where it stands, and what comes next. It is updated whenever the state changes.

In one page

What. A program that chooses the best move in Slay the Spire 2, a card game of hidden information: you do not know the draw order, the enemy's next move, or what the next room holds. The target is play better than the best human players at Ascension 10 (the game's hard mode with all ten modifiers), Acts 1 to 3, Ironclad first and then every character. A small public mod that shows the best in-fight play is a side product.

How. Search plus two learned networks. At each decision the AI imagines many possible hidden states, plays each forward on a copy of the game, and picks the move that does best on average. A policy network tells the search which moves to look at first; a value network estimates how a run is going. There is no language model anywhere in the player, and no end-to-end reinforcement learning: the search is the player, the networks only make it faster and sharper.

Why a copy of the game. The search needs to play millions of imagined fights an hour. The real game manages about a hundred decisions a second on this PC and cannot be forked. The copy is our own fork of an open-source re-implementation of the game's combat and run logic in C#. It is exact on 97% of combat steps and plays about a thousand decisions a second per core.

Why the real game still matters. A copy that is slightly wrong teaches slightly wrong play. So the real game, run headless with no window, is the judge. Every stage the current AI plays a fixed block of 500 real games. Its win rate and how far it gets are the headline numbers. Every real game it plays is also replayed on the copy, step by step. Where the two disagree, the disagreement is weighed by how often the AI meets it, and only the heavy disagreements get fixed. The copy is improved on demand, never on principle.

Where we are (2026-10-25). The loop runs unattended around the clock. Three generations of the AI have been promoted; on real games the mean floor reached moved from 11.3 to 12.7, with no wins yet at Ascension 10 (the Act 1 boss is beaten about 2% of the time). The lockstep comparison shows the copy disagrees with the game on 0.6% of the AI's decisions, and nine tenths of that disagreement sits in a few ordering mechanisms of the copy's combat core. That core is being rebuilt in the game's own shape; its first two mechanism families are migrated and switched on as of fork main cf74c742, with the proof passing every gate; the runtime the agent itself plays on does not run the new core yet (4 to 6 rounds). The diagnosis of the next family in line found that most of its weight was one single-site bug already fixed by the fix lane, so it is not migrated. The rebuild reached its 120-round stop line, and the owner set a new one: the native step first, so the AI plays on the new core, then a re-rank, then any further family only on a measured number. A GPU inference server lifted self-play throughput by a fifth. The GPU lane then found that the AI's run-value head, written off in week 3 as having no skill, has real skill once a duplication bug in the self-play records was fixed; the fix and the better training recipe are landed. A paired test then put that value head in charge of the run-level decisions (which path, rest or shop) and the same AI reached four more floors on average and killed the Act 1 boss five times as often, on 2,000 fresh seeds. The owner switched it on for every new candidate; the next generation to beat generation 3 on paired seeds will carry it onto the real game.

What comes next. Put the kernel into the native library so that self-play, promotion and the lockstep run on the new core (4 to 6 rounds, under way), re-rank the divergences with the switch on, and take any further family one at a time by its measured kernel share with a diagnosis first. The fix lane and the agent lane keep turning, and gate G1 falls at the end of week 4 on the re-ranked table.

The map: how the machine works

Everything in the project is one loop. The real game produces truth, the copy produces volume, and the comparison between them decides what to fix. The status dot on each box is today's state.

Status dots: green running, teal done, amber waiting on a result or a decision, grey later. Numbers on the boxes are the latest measured values (block S3 of 2026-10-24 for the comparer and the ranking; fork main cf74c742 for the tests; the GPU lane's window of 2026-10-25 for self-play).

Reading the map

The real game is the game's own code from the archived v0.107.1 build, run headless through a decision channel. It plays whole Ascension 10 runs at about 120 decisions a second across 12 processes. It never touches a player profile, never connects to the network, and runs only because the security review recorded a GO for it.

The lockstep comparer starts the copy from the same seed and feeds it every decision the AI made in the real game. After each step it compares the two states. A mismatch is logged as a divergence, weighed by how many AI decisions it touched and whether the run's outcome changed, and the copy is re-anchored from the real record so the comparison can continue.

The ranking groups divergences by the mechanism behind them and by hook family: the game's combat is a set of hooks (damage, death, card play, turn start, piles, rewards) that content listens to in a fixed order. On the latest block, 90% of the AI's divergence weight sits in those ordering families rather than in one-off bugs, and the top five families hold 97% of it. That number is what decided the shape of the rebuild.

The kernel rebuild and the fix lane both improve the copy, behind the same gates: 7,370 C# tests, a 4,392-fight recorded corpus that may never regress, a replay of 20,294 public Ironclad runs, a byte-for-byte proof that the rebuilt core and the old one produce identical states until a family is switched on, and one reviewer per change.

Self-play, training and the promotion gate are the flywheel. Twenty worker processes play the current AI against itself on the copy, with a shared GPU inference server answering the policy network's queries. The GPU trains the next candidate from that data. A candidate is promoted only if it reaches a better mean floor than the current generation on 2,000 fresh paired seeds, with a one-sided test at 1%, a guard that the Act 1 boss-kill rate did not get worse, and an audit. The promoted generation then plays the real game, and the loop closes.

How exact the copy is

The plan and why it is shaped this way

The plan of record was written on 2026-10-22 after an independent review of the previous approach, and is in force through decisions D17 to D21. It turned the project's order around.

What was wrong before

Until 2026-10-22 the project was perfecting the copy before building any AI, on the theory that an AI must not learn from a flawed copy. That order never ends. The remaining errors in the copy were architectural (the order in which the game runs its hooks, the queue it uses for actions), fixing them one by one did not converge, and a full rebuild of the combat core was priced at most of a week's credit. Meanwhile no AI existed and nothing measured the number the goal is judged by: how often the AI wins real games.

The four moves of the plan

  1. Build the AI now, on the copy we have. The copy is exact on 97% of combat steps and fast. The search and training code already existed upstream and only had to run here.
  2. Judge it on the real game. The game's own code runs headless on this PC and is exact by definition. Every stage the AI plays a fixed block of 500 real games.
  3. Let the AI say which copy errors matter. Every real game is replayed on the copy. Disagreements are ranked by how often the AI meets them. Only those get fixed. The rebuild of the core is sized from that ranking, not assumed.
  4. Keep the CPU busy and the credit idle. Self-play, training, scoring and comparison run around the clock as CPU jobs. Agent rounds (a Claude session doing one reviewed piece of work) consume only what those jobs prepare. The cheapest model that can do the job does it.

Principles that hold every day

  • Truth comes from the real game. The copy trains and searches; the referee judges.
  • Fidelity is demand-driven. Nothing is fixed because a random run found it, unless the AI meets it too.
  • Measure before building. Every build above about five rounds has a measured trigger, a stop rule and a re-plan point.
  • Land small, gate hard. One commit per change; reject only what a commit breaks; one reviewer per change.
  • Root causes. A divergence is fixed at its mechanism and at every other use of that mechanism, never at the symptom.
  • No new access. No network from game processes, no bot on the live client, no game files in any repository.

The kernel rebuild, explained

The problem. The game's combat is driven by hooks: when a card is played, when damage lands, when a creature dies, at the start and end of a turn. Dozens of cards, relics and powers listen to each hook, and the game runs the listeners in a fixed order through an action queue. The copy was written by hand, site by site, and gets the order right most of the time. Where it does not, a fight diverges in ways no single-site fix can close, because the fix for one card breaks the order for another.

The owner's preference was to rebuild the copy's dispatch in the game's own structure rather than keep patching orders one site at a time. The question was how much to rebuild.

Two tiers. Tier 2 is the full rebuild: every combat model re-expressed as handlers on a new core, every test converted, one switchover; reviewed at about 255 rounds, three quarters of a week. Tier 1 builds the same core (the hook dispatcher with the game's listener order, generated from a table that a trace patch verifies against the real game, plus the action queue) and then migrates content one hook family at a time, in the order of the AI's ranking, while the dispatcher calls the old code for families not yet migrated. It stops when what is left holds under 10% of the AI's weight. Estimated at 60 to 100 rounds.

Why Tier 1 won. The full price bundles a cheap kernel with an expensive re-expression of about a thousand content models, and three quarters of the AI's divergence sits in a few ordering families. Migrating those first buys most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost. The old objection to a hybrid ("never sound until it is done") is answered by having the real game as judge: soundness is measured by the transfer gap and per-family divergence, not assumed. When the ranking method was corrected on 2026-10-24, the top five families held 97% of the architectural weight, which made the choice decisive.

How a family is migrated

  1. Diagnose first. One Opus session reads the family's divergences and the game's method, and names the mechanism. By-family tags are heuristics; one family's apparent 65% share turned out to be a comparer artefact.
  2. Port every handler. Each (model, hook) pair in the family gets a handler on the new core, in batches with their tests. A port gate counts pairs with no handler, including models no recorded fight reaches; it must read 0, because a missing handler would silently drop under the switch.
  3. Prove nothing changed while off. A byte proof runs the whole recorded corpus (47,432 fight pairs in eleven modes) through old and new and requires identical states.
  4. Prove it is right when on. With the family switched on in a test fixture: the old test suites must not gain a failure; the fights traced against the real game must not get worse; the recorded corpus must not get worse; a strict step-order test and a fuzzer that compares combat endings must pass.
  5. Switch, then re-measure. The family is marked migrated; the lockstep comparison on the next real-game block measures the result.

What has happened

The core (dispatcher and queue) landed in week 2 and week 3 with its own tests. F08 and F10 were taken together because the kill sequence binds them: the game kills, runs death hooks and removes the creature inside each damage call. All their handlers were ported between 2026-10-24 and 2026-10-25: a cloud pilot batch and five cloud batches for powers and relics, local batches for monsters and the models that needed the game's context, a review that found and fixed a kernel bug (a search fork could advance the live run's random stream), and finally the eleven pairs no recorded fight reaches.

The switch-on proof then failed in four rounds of narrowing, each time finding a mechanism: the old combat ending fired at the kill while the game ends combat after its queue drains (113 fights); the game's two turn-start win checks had never run under the old turn start; a comparer census fault; two relics whose damage folds had never been ported. The corpus count of worse fights went 122, 3, 2, 0. On 2026-10-25 at 05:45Z the switch landed on fork main cf74c742 with every gate green: 7,383 C# tests, the old engine's byte proof at 0 differ, and with the switch on 71 corpus fights better, 4,321 the same and none worse. One caveat matters for the goal: the switch is live in the test engine and in the comparer's new-core mode, but the native runtime the agent and the lab play on does not build the kernel yet. The agent gains from the rebuild only once that runtime runs the new core. That native step landed on 2026-10-25 in two rounds: an engine option that defaults to the old engine, with every self-play and training entry point pinned to it, and then the search's combat handles, clones, forks, snapshot loads and screen answers routed through the kernel. The new core is slower in the agent's runtime: 24% fewer engine steps and 31% fewer search simulations per second than the old one, because the dispatcher does the game's full listener walk. So moving training onto it is a trade between fidelity and pace, decided by the owner on the re-rank.

The re-rank says the rebuild worked. On the same 500 real seeds, with generation 3 searching on the new core, the copy's disagreement with the game fell from 0.59% to 0.30% of the AI's decisions and the divergence weight fell 61%. The death family, which the new core now handles, lost 93% of its weight; damage lost 62%. The piles family (F11) barely moved, so it became the largest remaining family at 26%, and its diagnosis under the new rule found why: 86% of it is a measurement artefact in the referee driver, which sorts the two cards kept at one event into grid order where the game and the copy both keep pick order, after which the re-anchor never repairs the run deck's order and the same swap breaks later fights. The kernel would address at most 3.5% of F11, so it is not migrated; the driver fix is approved and the nightly block re-measures it. The AI's real-game floor on the new core was 12.98 against 12.72, within noise, which is the honest measure of how much the old core's errors were hurting it: not detectably, yet.

What this means for the rebuild. F06 was 85% a single-site rule already fixed; F11 is 86% a driver artefact. Neither was kernel work, and no further family is indicated by the measured weight. Tier 1 stands at F08 and F10 unless the re-rank after the driver fix shows otherwise. The rebuild lane is stopped for the week, with its remaining items (the parity harness, snapshot and re-anchor tests, the kernel's speed, three failed re-anchors and one odd screen readout) priced for week 4.

F06 is not migrated. Its diagnosis, done before any port as the rule requires, found that nearly every F06 record was a whole-turn offset where one side took a lone End Turn the other did not, and that 85% of the family's weight came from a single cause: the Fairy in a Bottle potion offered as a combat action, a single-site rule the fix lane had already corrected after the ranked block ran. Another 6% is an extra energy point after the same play, source unknown. The kernel would address at most that 6%, so migrating F06 (about 20 to 27 rounds for 125 handler pairs) would buy nothing measured. The family tags are heuristics, which is why a diagnosis comes first; this is the second time one has overturned a tag.

Tier 1 is at its stop line. The rebuild has spent about 120 to 125 rounds against the 120-round line set in D25 and the original estimate of 60 to 100: the kernel stage about 42, and F08 with F10 about 75 to 85 against an estimate of 15, the overrun being the switch-on proof surfacing kernel defects that cut across families. What is left is the native step (4 to 6 rounds, after which the switch can be measured by the AI's weight), then at most one further family after a re-rank, chosen by its measured kernel share with a diagnosis first. Per the rule the lane stopped and reported, and the owner set a new line (D31): the native step now, then the re-rank, then any further family only on its measured kernel share after a diagnosis. The kernel itself was worth building and is built; what was not worth doing was migrating F06 on a tag. The owner added the rule that follows from it: no migration is priced from a tag, only from a diagnosis that names the mechanism and counts the handler pairs; the tags choose what to diagnose, never what to build. The escalation rules to Tier 2 stand as written in the G1 rule.

Rules that never bend

  • The game's code runs headless only on this PC and only because the security review recorded GO. It never connects to the network, never touches a player profile, and no bot ever plays the live client, Daily runs or leaderboards.
  • No game file, asset or decompiled text goes into any repository, result file or commit. The public repositories hold our own code only.
  • The reviewed emulator checkout is read-only. Our changes live in our fork and are pushed after their tests pass; useful fixes are offered upstream as pull requests.
  • Results and logs carry no host names, user names or personal paths. No credential is stored on the PC.
  • The AI sees only what a player can see. Hidden information is resampled, never read; leak tests are gates.
  • No language model in the player; no end-to-end reinforcement learning; no learning from human run logs.
  • A decision is changed only by a new dated entry in the decision log. Overnight decisions are reversible by the owner.

How this paper is kept, and its change log

The orchestrator session republishes this page whenever the project's state changes: a landing, a switch, a promotion, a decision, a new measurement. Every figure names its date and the block or commit it comes from. The source file lives in the Spireline repository at docs/paper/spireline-paper.html, so any later session can update it.

    Glossary

    Round
    One reviewed piece of agent work (a worker plus its reviewer), about 60 tool calls for Sonnet; Opus counts 1.7.
    Referee
    The real game's code running headless as the judge.
    Emulator, the copy
    Our fork of the open-source re-implementation of the game's logic; fast and forkable.
    Lockstep
    The copy replaying a real game's decisions from the same seed, compared by state hash after each step.
    Hook family
    A group of game events that content listens to in a fixed order: F08 damage, F10 death and removal, F06 card play, F11 piles, F15 rewards, and so on.
    Tier 1
    The kernel in the game's shape plus families migrated in ranked order; the old code serves the rest meanwhile.
    Switch-on
    Marking a family as migrated so the new core handles it in real play; allowed only when every proof gate passes.
    Port gate, byte proof
    The count of (model, hook) pairs without a handler, which must be 0; and the corpus-wide check that old and new produce identical states while a family is off.
    Generation
    One trained candidate of the AI; promoted only by the gate.
    E-block, S-block
    Fixed fresh sets of seeds used once: E for promotion on the copy, S for scoring on the real game.
    Transfer gap, G
    The AI's win rate on the copy minus its win rate on the real game, on paired seeds. Zero so far because both are zero.
    Quiet window
    A reserved period when the CPU belongs to one measurement and no other heavy job starts.
    Allowance
    The subscription's weekly usage, read as a percentage on the usage page.
